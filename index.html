Peaceful protest guide

Protest is a right. These habits keep it lawful, safe and credible.

Article 51A(i) makes it a fundamental duty to safeguard public property and abjure violence. A protest that stays peaceful keeps both the law and public sympathy on its side.

        RTI and petitions

        Asking questions in writing is a lawful, peaceful way to hold institutions to account.

        Right to Information Act, 2005

        • Any citizen can ask a public authority for records or information.
        • Write to the authority's Public Information Officer (PIO). Central bodies can be reached online at rtionline.gov.in; states have their own portals or paper forms.
        • A small fee applies, and families below the poverty line are exempt. The fee differs by state.
        • A reply is due in 30 days, or 48 hours where life or liberty is at stake.
        • If unsatisfied: first appeal to the First Appellate Authority within 30 days, then second appeal to the Information Commission within 90 days.

        A sample RTI question

        • "For my assembly constituency, please give the number of names added and deleted from the electoral roll between [date] and [date], with the notice or order under which deletions were made."
        • Ask for documents and numbers, not opinions. Keep it short and specific.
        • Some information may be refused under the Act's exemptions, such as personal data. You can appeal.

        Petitions and complaints

        • Write to the Electoral Registration Officer, District Election Officer (usually the Collector) or Chief Electoral Officer. Keep a dated copy and proof of delivery.
        • General grievances against central government bodies can be filed on CPGRAMS (pgportal.gov.in).
        • You can also petition your MP or MLA, and approach the courts under Articles 32 and 226.

        Fees, portals and time limits change. Confirm on the official RTI portal or with legal aid.
